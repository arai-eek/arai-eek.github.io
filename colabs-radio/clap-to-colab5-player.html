About This Episode

In this episode, Darcy Neal shares her workshopology practice, listening in on invisible electromagnetic fields, and the power of shared communal maker spaces. Later, Yashas Shetty reflects on Ayudha Puja (giving thanks to one's tools and synthesizers), calls for opening up black boxes, and explains why true collaboration starts with simply getting a bottle of beer and jamming together.

Show Summary

In this fifth installment of Clap to COLAB, host Kaspar König dives into the physical, social, and political dimensions of tools, creative technology, and collaborative spaces. Creative technologist and workshopologist Darcy Neal discusses listening to invisible electromagnetic fields, organizing dynamic hardware labs with cafeteria trays, and the resilience of maker communities from Chiang Mai to Chaos Communication Camp. Later, artist and educator Yashas Shetty (co-founder of Hackteria and ISRO) reflects on the ancient Indian ritual of Ayudha Puja—giving thanks to one's tools and synthesizers—while unpacking the need to reclaim biohacking from Silicon Valley, unmasking non-neutral algorithms, and celebrating the raw, informal joy of jamming over bureaucratic grant-writing.

🎙️ Key Interviews & Segments

1. Darcy Neal on Electromagnetic Listening & Workshopology

Background: Darcy Neal is a creative technologist and self-described "workshopologist" working primarily with hardware electronics and interactive synthesizer controllers.
Listening to the Invisible: Darcy discusses her focus on exploring electromagnetic frequencies (EMF), building custom pickup coils that allow listeners to tap into the invisible electromagnetic soundscapes radiated by everyday gadgets and power grids.
Lab Organization in Flux: She shares her modular workspace system using cafeteria/baker's trays to manage half a dozen parallel hardware projects, keeping surface mount components cataloged in baseball card binder sleeves.

2. Darcy Neal on Collective Energy & Living Laboratories

The Importance of Community: Reflecting on the political and cultural challenges facing independent artists, Darcy stresses that physical community gatherings are more critical than ever.
Chiang Mai CoLab: She reflects on the transformative experience of living, working, and cooking together during the Technobiological Futures CoLab in Chiang Mai, surrounded by rich biodiversity.
Outdoor Soldering: Inspired by the massive soldering villages at Chaos Communication Camp (CCCamp 2023), Darcy highlights the joy and mental relief of taking electronic tinkering outside the four walls of the laboratory.

3. Yashas Shetty on Ayudha Puja & Tool Worship

Ayudha Puja: Speaking from Bangalore, Yashas describes the regional festival of Ayudha Puja—a day dedicated to blessing, cleaning, and offering gratitude to one's tools, instruments, and synthesizers.
Indian Sonic Research Organisation (ISRO): Yashas introduces ISRO, an open Bangalore-based collective and sound lab exploring experimental music, open workshops, and artist residencies.
Founding Hackteria (2009): Yashas recounts the genesis of the Hackteria network in 2009 with Marc Dusseiller and Andy Garcia, driven by open questions about what it means to collaborate across science and art.

4. Yashas Shetty on Reclaiming Biohacking & Seizing the Machine

What Grows in Labs: When asked what thrives in laboratories, Yashas answers: "People grow best in our labs." The core purpose of a laboratory is human transformation and shared ideas.
Reclaiming Biohacking: Yashas critiques the co-optation of biohacking by Silicon Valley into an elitist pursuit of personal immortality and optimization. He advocates reclaiming technobiology as an open, collective, and socially just human endeavor rooted in historical alchemy.
Opening Black Boxes & The Luddite Spirit: Machines and algorithms are not neutral; they carry cultural biases and institutional agendas. Yashas re-evaluates the historical Luddites not as anti-technology, but as master machine operators who understood their tools and sought to seize control rather than submit to exploitation.

5. The Joy of Jamming vs. Grant-Writing

Jamming Culture: In a memorable closing reflection, Yashas contrasts institutional collaboration with musical jam sessions. While architects and academics often spend months drafting grant applications before ever working together, musicians simply say: "Bring a bottle of beer to my room and let's jam." True DIWO collaboration begins with low-barrier social presence and human connection.

🤖 About the Production Process

This project is an exploration of human-AI collaboration. The original radio broadcasts (approx. 3 hours of audio) were transcribed using Deepgram Nova-2 cloud speech recognition and refined with human editorial oversight.

Those transcripts were then processed by Google's Gemini AI to extract the chapter timelines, resolve speaker identities, and generate the episode summaries. Finally, the entire web experience—from the custom audio players to the 'glassmorphism' UI design—was collaboratively coded using the Antigravity AI coding assistant pair-programming with the creator.

Approximate Compute & Ecological Footprint:

  • Token Usage: ~1,200,000 input tokens (due to the AI repeatedly reading 55,000+ words of transcripts in context) and ~35,000 output tokens (writing the code, HTML, and summaries).
  • Estimated Compute Cost: ~$4.50 USD (equivalent API cost for the LLM).
  • Energy Consumption: ~0.35 kWh (Deepgram audio transcription + Cloud AI inference) — Roughly equivalent to boiling a kettle of water 3.5 times.
